S1The Program at a Glance1972–1995 · the
psychic-intelligence era

For twenty-three years, the United States government paid scientists and soldiers to do the impossible: describe secret sites on the far side of the world using nothing but coordinates and consciousness. The effort went by seven codenames, spent roughly $20 million, employed about 40 people including ~23 remote viewers, and ended with an outside review whose two authors disagreed on almost everything except one point — it had never produced actionable intelligence.

S2Origins: Cold War & SRI1972–1977
the lab years

The program began not as a military unit but as a physics experiment — and not because Americans believed in psychics, but because they feared the Soviets might have them.

Declassified program histories record that U.S. intelligence tracked an extensive Soviet parapsychology effort: twenty or more research centers with an estimated annual budget around $21 million. In 1972, laser physicists Harold Puthoff and Russell Targ began exploratory work at Stanford Research Institute in Menlo Park on both informational psi (remote viewing, precognition) and causal psi (psychokinesis). Their early results — and the Soviet reports — drew CIA funding.

The methodological leap came in 1973: instead of sending a "beacon" person to the target, viewers were given only geographic coordinates. Ingo Swann and Pat Price named it Scanate — scanning by coordinate. It was outrageous on its face, which is exactly why the sponsors took it seriously: coordinates eliminated the obvious sensory channels. Within a year, that protocol would be aimed at the Soviet Union's most secret weapons laboratory.

Why the coordinates mattered Every later CRV session inherits the Scanate design: a target reduced to numbers the viewer cannot reason from. Whatever you conclude about psi, the experimental hygiene — blind targets, no cueing, structure instead of narrative — was invented here, not at Fort Meade.
S3The Fort Meade Unit1978–1991
from detachment to directorate

Army intelligence first formalized the effort in 1976–77 as Gondola Wish, an evaluation of adversary applications under Dale Graff, then as an operational detachment: Grill Flame, INSCOM Detachment G, in buildings 2560 and 2561 at Fort Meade — later remembered as "an old, leaky wooden barracks."

Sept 1979

Grill Flame's first operational mission locates an aircraft crash site within about fifteen miles — enough to push the unit from training to operational status.

5 Mar 1980

Undersecretary William Perry terminates R&D funding for psychoenergetics; the decision is reversed in 1983.

1983

SRI research folds into the unit as INSCOM Center Lane. The same summer, LTC McDonnell writes the Gateway assessment (companion site).

1984

The National Research Council delivers an unfavorable evaluation for the Army Research Institute; Jack Anderson reports the program publicly; a September MOA prepares the move to DIA.

1985–86

Army funding ends; the unit becomes Sun Streak under DIA's Scientific and Technical Intelligence Directorate (DT-S), formally transferring in January 1986.

1991

Contract work passes from SRI to SAIC and the whole enterprise is rechristened STAR GATE, downgraded from a Special Access Program to limited dissemination (LIMDIS). Edwin May's group ends up producing most of the late data.

S4The Method: CRV Stages I–VIMcNear manual
Feb 1985

The 1985 working paper by project officer Tom McNear — Coordinate Remote Viewing Stages I–VI and Beyond — is the program's technical core. It teaches a discipline: coordinates in, ideograms out, and a running war against the brain's urge to invent the answer.

StageNameWhat happens on paper
IMajor GestaltThe ideogram: an instant kinesthetic squiggle — A (feeling/motion, e.g. "fluid/wavy") and B (automatic analytic response, e.g. "water"). Visual images get labeled AOL immediately.
IISensory ContactNon-visual signal: sounds, smells, tastes, textures, temperatures, energies. Still no pictures allowed.
IIIDimension, Motion, MobilityAperture widens: dimensionals (tall, thin, 350 ft), the aesthetic impact ("WOW — this place makes me feel…"), dotted autonomic trackers, sketches.
IVGeneral Qualitative AspectsThe operational threshold. A hand-written matrix — S-2 · D · AI · EI · T · I · AOL · A/S — covering activities, objects, and the emotions of people at the site.
VSpecific Analytical AspectsInterrogating the signal line: extracting specifics without fabricating. Also the repair stage — looking through an AOL to find the real data that caused it.
VI3-D Contact & ModelingPhysical models of the site.
Exhibit S-B — The CRV signal modelafter McNear 1985, fig. 2SOURCE: MANUAL ↗

Session control is equally codified: the monitor supplies only date/time, coordinates, and a narrow feedback vocabulary — Site (done), C (correct), PC (probably correct), N (near), CFB (can't feedback) — and never gives negative feedback mid-session. A written summary closes every operational session. McNear's warning stands as the whole philosophy in one line: "One cannot expect to learn RV simply by reading this document. CRV must be learned by doing."

S7The 1995 EvaluationUtts vs Hyman
and the kill shot

In June 1995 the CIA's Office of Research and Development contracted the American Institutes for Research to evaluate the program. Two outside experts read the evidence and wrote, effectively, two different reports.

Utts: "Using the standards applied to any other area of science, it is concluded that psychic functioning has been well established. The statistical results of the studies examined are far beyond what is expected by chance." — and yet the effects are too small and inconsistent for intelligence gathering.

Hyman: "The case for psychic functioning is better than it ever has been… But the evidence is still too weak and too inconsistent to be used as evidence for psi." The overwhelming mass of session data is "vague, general, and way off target."

AIR, An Evaluation of Remote Viewing, 29 Sept 1995 (GWU NSAEBB438/doc_57)

What they agreed on, and what decided the issue: no demonstrated operational utility. AIR's operational conclusion is the sentence that ended the program: no remote-viewing report ever provided actionable information for any intelligence operation.

The chronology worth knowing: the CIA ordered the closure on 30 June 1995 — three months before the report was published on 29 September. A 2023 AFIO thesis calls it a "rush to prejudgement" and notes AIR reviewed about ten experiments, not the thousands of operational sessions. The archive editors add their own counterweight: 17 of the 19 agencies that tasked viewers kept coming back. Both things are true: the review was narrow, and the operational record was still not good enough.

S9Practice & Presetstry the structure,
not the mythology

The Ft. Meade protocol used no audio at all — CRV is paper, blind targets, and discipline. If you want to train it, that is where the evidence points; sound is only an optional settling bed, and the declassified record assigns it no role.

RV-01A CRV-style home session (45–60 min)with a partner
  1. Target officer (partner): seal a photo or location name in an envelope; label it only with arbitrary coordinates or a code number. You never see it.
  2. Settle for 5 minutes (optional bed below), pen and blank paper ready, no screens.
  3. Stage I: read the code; let the first squiggle happen. Write A (feeling/motion) and B (automatic response). Any picture that arrives gets labeled AOL and set aside.
  4. Stage II: list non-visual sensations in columns — sound, smell, texture, temperature, energy.
  5. Stage III: dimensionals only (tall/wide/distance). If an aesthetic impact hits, take the AI break, name the feeling, then continue.
  6. Stage IV: write the matrix S-2 · D · AI · EI · T · I · AOL · A/S and interrogate each column. Do not analyze as you go.
  7. Summarize in plain language. Only then open the envelope. Score hits, misses, and unverifiables separately — unverifiables are not hits.
  8. Log 20 sessions before judging yourself. The misses teach more than the hits; that is what "structure" means.
